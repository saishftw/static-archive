INDIA: 75 YEARS OF
GOVERNMENT ECONOMIC PERFORMANCE

Eleven macroeconomic indicators compared across nine Indian governments. Growth series are compound annual growth rates (CAGR) over each term; share and level series are term averages. Source figures as compiled from World Bank data on Past Performance — AndhBhakt.org.

Dashes (—) mark indicators with no recorded observation for that term and are excluded from every chart and ranking.

1 / MASTER CROSS-METRIC HEATMAP

Cells show the exact recorded figure. Colour encodes the value normalised within each indicator (0 = weakest government on that metric, 1 = strongest). Average Inflation and Debt/GDP are inverted, so lower values read green. Greener is always better.

2 / INDICATOR SMALL MULTIPLES

HIGHER IS BETTER — GROWTH RATES (CAGR %)

One panel per indicator, each on its own vertical scale. Plotting Real GDP (roughly 5–7%) and the stock market (4–17%) on a single shared axis compresses the first into near-identical bars; separating them preserves the detail in both. Governments stay in chronological order and keep the same colour throughout the report, so no legend is needed.

HIGHER IS BETTER — OPENNESS & INVESTMENT (% OF GDP)

Exports, imports and FDI are levels, not growth rates. Bars drawn from zero make 20.84% and 22.17% look identical; a dot plot puts the eye on the gap between the values rather than on the length of the bar.

LOWER IS BETTER — INFLATION & DEBT (%)

Average inflation and average debt-to-GDP, sorted best to worst rather than chronologically, because the question these answer is a ranking question. Debt is only on record from 1991.

3 / TRAJECTORY OVER TIME

The nine governments form a time series, not an unordered set of categories. Bar charts discard that ordering; a slope chart restores it. Read this to see whether a shift was a step change under one government or a continuous ramp that several governments rode — trade openness, for example, climbs steadily from the 1991 reforms onward rather than jumping under any single term.

4 / DEVIATION FROM THE PERIOD AVERAGE

The same figures, re-baselined so that zero is the average across all governments with a recorded observation for that indicator. This answers "above or below par?" instead of "how big?". Bars to the right are better than average after accounting for indicator direction, so a low-inflation term shows green to the right.

5 / MODERN ERA BREAKDOWN

Vajpayee (1998–04), Manmohan Singh (2004–14) and Modi (2014–25) have near-complete observations across all eleven indicators, allowing a direct like-for-like comparison. The parallel-coordinates view normalises each axis within these three governments only (top = best of the three, with inflation and debt inverted). It replaces the radar chart that previously sat here: area on a radar changes if you reorder the axes, which makes "bigger blob = better" a visual illusion rather than a finding.

6 / OVERALL LEADERBOARD

Every indicator is normalised to a 0–1 scale within itself (0 = weakest government on record for that indicator, 1 = strongest), with inflation and debt inverted so that higher always means better. A government's raw score is the average of its normalised scores across the indicators it has an observation for, scaled to 100. All eleven indicators are weighted equally — a deliberate choice, and an arguable one.

The shock-adjusted score repeats the exercise on a counterfactual in which the average annual drag from documented exogenous shocks is added back to the four real-output growth series (GDP, GDP per capita, manufacturing, services). Policy-induced events — demonetisation, the GST rollout, the Pokhran-II sanctions — are deliberately not adjusted away, because the consequences of a government's own choices are exactly what this is measuring.

Read this before quoting the ranking

    Unequal coverage is the single biggest weakness of this ranking. Manmohan Singh's term has all eleven indicators on record; Nehru's and Indira Gandhi's first term have seven. Averaging over whatever happens to exist means the governments are not being scored on the same exam. The stock-market indicator exists for only three terms and is won decisively by one of them, which alone moves the top of the table.

    7 / THE SHOCK LEDGER

    The full list of events behind the adjustment, published so the choices can be argued with. Bars show the estimated percentage points of real GDP growth lost, spread across the length of each term. Green marks a tailwind — the 2014–16 collapse in crude prices was a genuine windfall for a large net oil importer and is counted as such. Rows marked POLICY are shown but excluded from the adjustment.

    8 / BENCHMARKED AGAINST THE WORLD

    A second, independent way to strip out global conditions: compare each government's real GDP growth to average world growth over the same years. Growing at 6% while the world grew at 5% is a very different achievement from growing at 6% while the world grew at 2.5%. This measure needs no estimate of any individual shock — global downturns are already inside the benchmark — which makes it a useful cross-check on the shock adjustment above.

    World growth figures are approximate period averages derived from World Bank and IMF world aggregates. They are indicative, not precise, and should be read at the level of "strong tailwind / strong headwind" rather than to two decimal places.

    9 / DATA PROVENANCE & VALIDATION

    The indicator figures are reproduced from a compilation published on AndhBhakt.org, itself sourced from World Bank data. That site publishes from an explicitly political standpoint. That does not make its numbers wrong, but it does mean they should be treated as a claim to be checked rather than as a primary source — which is why the checks below are published alongside them.

    What checks out

    • Manmohan Singh's 6.75% GDP CAGR sits squarely on the ~6.8% commonly reported for 2004–14 on the 2011–12 series.
    • Modi's 5.99% is inside the 5.9–6.2% range usually cited for 2014–24 once the COVID contraction is included.
    • The debt figures of 73–79% of GDP are consistent with general government debt, which the IMF put at about 81% of GDP in 2023. They are far above central-government-only debt of roughly 57%, so the series is general government.
    • Desai's near-zero 0.09% GDP CAGR reflects the FY1979–80 contraction of about 5%, which is well documented and is one of the deepest in India's post-independence record.
    • The export and import shares tracking from roughly 4% of GDP in the early 1960s to a low-20s peak in the early 2010s matches the well-established post-1991 opening.

    What to treat with caution

    • Vajpayee's 6.16% GDP CAGR runs above the ~5.7–5.8% average usually quoted for 1998–2004; the gap is sensitive to whether calendar or fiscal years are used and to the exact endpoints.
    • Growth series straddle the 2011–12 base-year revision, whose back-series is genuinely contested. Figures either side of it are not perfectly comparable.
    • "Inflation" is not defined in the source. India has WPI, CPI-IW, combined CPI and the GDP deflator, and they can differ by several percentage points in the same year.
    • Three terms are absent entirely: Shastri (1964–66), V. P. Singh / Chandra Shekhar (1989–91) and Deve Gowda / Gujral (1996–98). Some of the worst shock years fall in those gaps and are therefore attributed to nobody.
    • Desai's 16.73% revenue CAGR and −2.20% per-capita CAGR are two-year rates. They are arithmetically correct and analytically close to meaningless.
    • A full row-by-row reconciliation against the World Bank's World Development Indicators could not be completed here; the checks above are against published summaries rather than against the raw series.
